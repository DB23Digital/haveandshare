Executive Self-Doubt: Why Capable Leaders Doubt Themselves Most

Claudina Hafenscher

COMENSA Accredited Senior Coach & Certified PQ® Coach

11 August 2026 8 min read

Why do capable executives struggle with self-doubt?

Executive self-doubt persists because seniority removes the feedback that used to reassure you. The higher you rise, the fewer people tell you the truth, the more ambiguous the decisions become, and the more your worth becomes tangled up with your last result. Capability does not switch it off. Left unexamined, it grows quietly behind a track record that looks, from the outside, like the opposite problem.

Nobody schedules a meeting to tell a CEO they seem unsure of themselves. That is precisely the problem. The higher you climb, the less feedback you receive, and the more of your internal state you have to read entirely on your own. Executive self-doubt does not announce itself the way it might in a junior role, as a visible hesitation or a question asked out loud. It goes underground, and it takes the form of over-preparation, delayed decisions, and a persistent private sense that you are one bad quarter away from being found out.

This is different from imposter syndrome, though the two overlap heavily. The American Psychological Association defines impostor phenomenon as a persistent inability to internalise success despite objective evidence, usually paired with a specific fear of exposure. Executive self-doubt is broader: it is the accumulated weight of making consequential decisions with incomplete information, carrying other people's outcomes, and doing all of it with almost no one positioned to reassure you that you are doing it well.

Why seniority makes it worse, not better

There is a common assumption that self-doubt fades as a career progresses, replaced by hard-won confidence. In practice, the opposite often happens, for three reasons.

The feedback thins out. Junior roles come with performance reviews, direct managers, and colleagues at a similar level who will tell you plainly when something is off. Executive roles come with a board that wants confidence, not doubt, a team that filters what they say upward, and peers who are managing exactly the same performance around their own uncertainty. The honest signal that used to calibrate your self-assessment quietly disappears.

The decisions get harder to verify. A junior analyst can usually check their work against a clear right answer. A CEO deciding whether to enter a new market, restructure a division, or back a struggling executive is making a judgment call with no clean answer key. Good outcomes take months or years to show up, which means the self-doubt has nothing but time to compound while you wait to find out if you were right.

The isolation is structural, not personal. It is not that senior leaders are worse at building relationships. It is that the role itself removes the people who could once double as a reality check. Direct reports have their own incentives. Peers are competitors as often as allies. That is precisely why an outside coach, someone with nothing to gain from managing your confidence for you, becomes useful at this level in a way it was not before.

The behaviours it produces

Executive self-doubt rarely looks like doubt. It looks like a set of behaviours that read, from the outside, as diligence or high standards.

Decision delay dressed up as thoroughness. Requesting one more round of data, one more stakeholder view, one more model, past the point where more information would actually change the decision.

Over-control of things that do not need it. Reviewing work that a competent team has already done well, not because it needs reviewing, but because letting go of it feels like relinquishing the one thing that proves your value.

Difficulty naming what you actually think in the room. Deferring to consensus, hedging a strong view, or waiting to see where others land before committing to a position, even when your judgment is exactly what the room needs.

None of these looks like self-doubt on a performance review. All of them are self-doubt, wearing the costume of competence.

What actually shifts it

Standard advice, believe in yourself, focus on your wins, does not work at this level, because it treats the problem as a thinking error rather than a whole-system pattern. In mBIT terms, the self-doubt lives in the gut brain, the centre responsible for identity and self-preservation, and it cannot be argued out of position by the head brain alone. That is why executives who are perfectly capable of building a rational case for their own competence still feel, physically, like frauds in the room.

Through Positive Intelligence coaching, we work with the specific Saboteur pattern driving it, usually the Judge turned inward, often paired with the Hyper-Achiever or the Controller. Naming the pattern precisely, rather than treating it as generic anxiety, is what makes it interceptable in the moment it shows up, in the boardroom, not just in retrospect.

The work is not about eliminating uncertainty. Senior decisions are genuinely uncertain, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of dishonesty. The work is building the capacity to make a clear decision and hold it, without the decision itself being contaminated by a fear that has nothing to do with the facts on the table.

If you are wondering whether this applies to you

It usually applies if you recognise the gap between how you are perceived and how you actually feel making the decisions that earn that perception. If your team would describe you as decisive and you would describe yourself as guessing well under pressure. If congratulations on a result land oddly, more relief than pride. If you have never said any of this out loud to anyone at work, because there is genuinely no one at work it would be safe to say it to. It is also more common than the silence around it suggests — Harvard Business Review research puts the proportion of high achievers who experience it at close to seven in ten.

That last point is the reason coaching exists as a category separate from mentorship or therapy. It gives you exactly one relationship where the goal is your clarity, not managing you, not being managed by you, and not diagnosing you.
