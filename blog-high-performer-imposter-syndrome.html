The High Performer Who Feels Like a Fraud: Why Success Doesn't Cure Imposter Syndrome

Claudina Hafenscher

COMENSA Accredited Senior Coach & Certified PQ® Coach

11 August 2026 7 min read

Why doesn't success cure imposter syndrome?

Success doesn't cure imposter syndrome because the Hyper-Achiever pattern that drives it treats every win as temporary proof, not permanent evidence. The mind resets to zero after each achievement instead of banking it, which is why the next promotion, award, or result never quite lands the way you expect it to.

There is a specific kind of disappointment that only high performers experience: the promotion arrives, the award is announced, the deal closes, and the feeling you expected, relief, pride, arrival, does not show up. What shows up instead is a flicker of satisfaction that fades within days, replaced by the next target and the same quiet suspicion that you got away with something.

If you have lived this more than once, you already know the standard advice does not work. "You'll feel it once you achieve X" was a lie the last three times. There is a reason for that, and it is not a character flaw — the APA Dictionary of Psychology describes this exact pattern as an inability to internalise success despite objective evidence of competence.

The achievement treadmill, explained properly

In Positive Intelligence terms, this pattern has a name: the Hyper-Achiever Saboteur. It convinces you, quietly and constantly, that your value as a person is conditional on your latest result. Not your character, not your relationships, not who you are when nobody is measuring you. Your latest result.

The mechanism is simple and brutal. Each achievement gets consumed almost immediately as evidence that the bar was not actually that high, or that you got lucky, or that anyone in your position would have done the same. The Hyper-Achiever does not let the win count as proof of competence. It treats the win as the new minimum, and starts measuring you against it right away.

This is why more success does not produce more security. It produces a higher bar and the same underlying doubt, now with more at stake if you fail to clear it.

Why this is worse for people who are actually good at their jobs

There is a cruel irony here that genuinely talented people run into more than mediocre ones. If you are skilled, you can usually trace exactly how a result was achieved, which effort, which decision, which relationship you leveraged. That visibility, which should be reassuring, instead becomes fuel for the doubt: "I know precisely how I did that, and none of it required anything special. Anyone could have."

Less skilled people, paradoxically, are sometimes protected from this by the Dunning-Kruger effect: they cannot see the gaps in their own competence clearly enough to doubt it. High performers see everything, including every place where things could have gone differently, and the Judge Saboteur uses that visibility against them. There is a strange upside worth naming here — Harvard Business Review research found that the same self-doubt often makes people more attentive and effective with others, even as it works against how they see themselves.

What actually breaks the cycle

Not more achievement. That has already been tried, repeatedly, by the person reading this. What breaks the cycle is intercepting the Hyper-Achiever in the specific moment it discounts a win, before the discounting becomes automatic and invisible.

Concretely, that means three things, practiced consistently rather than understood once:

Naming the pattern in real time. The moment you notice yourself explaining away a success, pause and label it: "That is my Hyper-Achiever telling me this doesn't count." Labelling it separates your identity from the automatic response, which is the first thing that has to happen before anything else can shift.

Building an evidence file you are not allowed to argue with. Not a mental tally, which the Hyper-Achiever will always find a way to discount, but a written record: what you did, what the actual outcome was, what it required of you. When the doubt arrives, you go to the record instead of relitigating the case from memory, where the Saboteur always wins.

Practicing daily mental fitness, not occasional reflection. This is where PQ® Reps come in: brief, repeated exercises that shift the brain out of Saboteur territory and into the Sage, the part of your mind capable of registering a win as a win. Research on the programme shows measurable shifts typically within six to eight weeks of consistent daily practice. It is a trained skill, not an epiphany.

This is not the same conversation as burnout

It is worth being precise here, because the two often get collapsed into one another. Burnout is what happens when the body and mind run out of capacity. The Hyper-Achiever pattern described here is what happens when capacity is intact but the internal scoring system never lets you register a result as sufficient. You can be nowhere near burnt out and still be running on this treadmill for years, which is exactly why it goes unaddressed for so long: from the outside, and often from the inside too, it looks like drive.
